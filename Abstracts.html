Andrew Heiss

Ph.D., Assistant Professor of International Politics and Policy, Georgetown University in Qatar

Friction Worth Keeping: What Keeps Us Human When AI Makes Everything Easy

Date & Time: November 10 at 8:00 AM, Eastern

Format: Opening Keynote (45 minutes)

Abstract

Generative AI removes friction. Need a poster for an event? Slides for stakeholders? An R package to access an API? An answer for a problem set? A feature requiring major refactoring? It’s all a prompt away. Incidental, frustrating friction is disappearing.

But not all friction is bad. Some friction is formative and central to the human experience. Struggling to learn develops skills and expertise. Creating things slowly gives us time to reflect and develop taste. Working through hard problems builds understanding and judgment. Contributing to a community builds trust.

AI can eliminate all kinds of friction, producing output that looks polished and convincing whether it’s grounded in human understanding or hollow underneath. How can we tell what friction is worth keeping? This talk explores the difference between friction that gets in our way and friction that makes us who we are, and what keeps us human when our work becomes effortless.

Bio

Andrew Heiss is an assistant professor of international politics and policy at the Edmund A. Walsh School of Foreign Service at Georgetown University in Qatar. Before moving to Qatar, he was an assistant professor at the Andrew Young School of Policy Studies at Georgia State University. He received his Ph.D. in public policy and political science from Duke University’s Sanford School of Public Policy in 2017.

His research examines how international NGOs work in authoritarian countries. He received the 2016–2018 Emerging Scholar Dissertation Award from the International Society for Third Sector Research (ISTR). He also works in public administration and policy, nonprofit management, international relations, and comparative politics.

He teaches quantitative research methods and data science, program evaluation and causal inference, data visualization, economics, and science communication. He is a certified RStudio instructor and a Posit Academy mentor.

Sahana Prasad

Guest Faculty, BITS-WILP, Pilani (India)

From Statistical Models to Generative AI: Why Statistical Thinking Still Matters in the R+AI Era

Date & Time: November 10 at 8:50 AM, Eastern

Format: Talk (35 minutes)

Topic: Evaluation, Observability, Responsible AI, and Governance

Co-Presenter: Sharanya Prasad, Senior Product Manager, Amazon Web Services

Abstract

Generative AI is rapidly changing how we work with data and write analytical code, including within the R ecosystem. Yet, as AI becomes increasingly capable of generating code, models, visualizations, and analytical narratives, the need for statistical thinking becomes more important—not less.

This talk explores how foundational statistical principles can guide the responsible and effective use of Generative AI in data analysis with R. Through practical examples, we will examine situations in which AI-generated analyses can produce plausible but misleading conclusions, including issues related to data quality, sampling, uncertainty, correlation and causation, model assumptions, bias, and interpretation.

The session will demonstrate how R can serve not merely as a coding environment, but as a tool for independently validating, reproducing, and critically evaluating AI-generated analytical results. We will also consider how the roles of statisticians and data analysts are evolving as AI increasingly participates in the analytical workflow.

The central message is simple: AI can generate code and analytical insights, but statistical thinking remains essential for determining whether those insights are valid, meaningful, and fit for decision-making.

Bio

She teaches and writes about data science and statistics using R, and has mentored researchers and trained faculty in applied analytics. Her books have been published by Springer Nature and Apress. She is also a recipient of the World Bank Statistical Capacity Building Award. Her work rests on one conviction: statistics earns its keep only when people can understand it, use it and trust it. She regularly publishes in Indian newspapers on varied topics.

Turtles All the Way Down: Recursive Language Models in R

Date & Time: November 10 at 12:05 PM, Eastern

Format: Talk (35 minutes)

Topic: Agents, MCP, and Tool Ecosystems

Abstract

corteza is an LLM agent harness on CRAN. It answers the question, “What if we put an LLM in the R console instead of the command line?” In the literature, CodeAct and Recursive Language Models name this shape; in the market, Prime Agent.

The test case is ARC-AGI-3. With one prompt, corteza scored an official 98.6% on the public set with Claude Opus 5 and 100% with GPT-6 Astra, ahead of Prime Agent’s 95.5%. ARC’s adapter also scores 100% with Astra, but the metrics hide that corteza’s run was better. I’ll show the 894-helper workspace the model built mid-game.

The second recursion is the development loop. This experiment gave corteza a lessons store and thorough tracing of every R call, a dataset about the harness. saber gives an agent the same view of corteza’s code. Next, corteza analyzes data about its own harness and changes it.

Date & Time: November 10 at 12:45 PM, Eastern

Format: Talk (35 minutes)

Topic: Agents, MCP, and Tool Ecosystems

Abstract

When you build a Shiny application with Golem, you can structure it a lot like an R package. Ten Agents, 8GB of RAM, and Ten Dollars a Month

Date & Time: November 10 at 1:25 PM, Eastern

Format: Lightning talk (15 minutes)

Topic: Agents, MCP, and Tool Ecosystems

Abstract

Ten AI agents run the back office of my consultancy. They track the market, draft content, triage my inbox and supervise each other, mostly while I sleep.

They run on a refurbished office desktop. 8GB of RAM, two hundred euros, five to ten dollars a month. The spec forbids Docker, Redis and any cloud database. The constraint did the design work.

Anyone can stand up agents now. Far fewer people talk about night forty, when the agents have been faithfully writing to a shared memory nobody is tidying and three quarters of it is repetition.

Three decisions determine whether a system like this still earns its keep a month in, and none of them are about the models. Where the memory lives. Who cleans it up. What establishes a fact before a model is allowed to interpret it.

No code on screen, no R required, no prior agent experience assumed. If you decide where AI goes in your organization, these are your decisions too.

Imad El Badisy

Research Engineer in Biostatistics, Mohammed VI Center for Research and Innovation (CM6RI)

Agentic Compactness: Measuring the Cost of AI-Generated Machine Learning Workflows in R

Date & Time: November 10 at 1:45 PM, Eastern

Format: Talk (35 minutes)

Topic: AI-Assisted R Development and Coding Workflows

Abstract

As AI coding agents increasingly build and repair machine learning workflows, the design of an R framework affects more than developer convenience. This talk introduces agentic compactness: the operational simplicity of a workflow measured through code length, framework calls, functions, objects, and package dependencies. Using matched classification and regression tasks, I compare funcml, mlr3, and tidymodels under shared learners, hyperparameters, data splits, validation, and scoring. The workflows achieve identical predictive performance but differ substantially in structural complexity, loading time, and memory use. A clinical genomics application demonstrates how funcml’s seven-verb interface supports an extended analysis. The talk concludes by showing how this benchmark can be extended to autonomous coding agents using first-run success, repair loops, and token consumption.

Bio

He builds open-source software for machine learning and computational biostatistics, turning advanced statistical methods into reliable tools that researchers can use. He holds a PhD from Aix-Marseille University (SESSTIM) on missing data and machine learning for survival analysis.

His research covers scientific machine learning, survival analysis, missing data imputation, and agentic data science: LLM-based agents that plan and run statistical workflows, and software designed so these agents can use it correctly, reproducibly, and safely. Nykko Vitali

PhD Student, Department of Psychology, Harvard University

Utilizing Reproducible Agents in RStudio - A Researcher’s Toolkit

Date & Time: November 10 at 3:00 PM, Eastern

Format: Talk (35 minutes)

Topic: Agents, MCP, and Tool Ecosystems

Abstract

ClaudeR is an R package and MCP server that connects AI agents to a live RStudio session.

The agent can code alongside you, act autonomously, guide you as you learn, edit select portions, annotate data, or check code claims against a manuscript—to name a few features. Every line it runs is logged and associated to the agent that ran it, and checkpoints make any step the agent takes reversible. Several agents from different sources (Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity, Cursor, etc.) can share one session and coordinate through a consensus gate, or independent agents can work silo’d in parallel. Whatever your workflow is, the goal of this package is to accommodate it. This talk covers the architecture, how to use it, some example workflows, and what building it taught me about working with agents.

Bio

Nykko Vitali is a PhD student in the Psychology Department at Harvard University. Nykko’s research focuses on decision making under uncertainty in environments where LLMs may be present. He also examines the behaviors, both good and bad, that have seeped into models through pre- and post-training. Nykko built ClaudeR shortly after Anthropic released the Model Context Protocol (MCP), to explore agentic coding within a controlled harness. A year and a half later, the package supports auditable autonomous coding workflows, multi-agent coordination, manuscript reproducibility checks, and data annotation, among other tools for researchers. It now works with Python and Positron as well as R and RStudio, and has 350 GitHub stars. The goal throughout has been to let any MCP-capable LLM work inside RStudio while keeping an auditable script of what was done and when.

Abstract

How do you move an R+AI workflow from a successful experiment into something a regulated organization can actually trust and operate in production? This talk presents a practical, reusable framework for preserving traceability, governance, reproducibility, and control as AI moves into higher-consequence workflows. We will use a deployment spectrum, from public AI services and enterprise APIs through private cloud, on-premises, and air-gapped environments, to show the trade-offs across control, speed, complexity, and cost. The goal is not maximum sovereignty, but choosing the level of control that matches the workload’s risk, value, and scale. Drawing on lessons from regulated analytics modernization and real production workflows, we will show how model selection, evaluation, observability, guardrails, validation, and human oversight fit together in practice. Attendees will leave with a reusable framework and readiness checklist for deciding what to control, validate, monitor, and govern before scaling R+AI.

Beyond the prompt

Date & Time: November 11 at 3:15 PM, Eastern

Format: Closing Keynote (45 minutes)

Abstract

AI tools can generate a polished ggplot, scaffold a Quarto report, and debug a dplyr pipeline in seconds. Should students still learn to do these things themselves? I argue yes—and that teaching programming and reproducible workflows is more important than ever. When AI produces plausible code freely, students must be able to assess whether it works as intended, recognize when an analysis quietly goes wrong, and structure their work so every step can be verified. Programming also teaches students to think with data: to ask sharper questions, understand what data can and cannot answer, and reason rigorously about uncertainty and models. Drawing on experience designing introductory data science courses, this talk reframes programming as a pathway to statistical thinking rather than syntax acquisition. Through concrete examples, it explores assignments and assessments that reward genuine understanding and prepare students to use AI critically, reproducibly, and responsibly.
